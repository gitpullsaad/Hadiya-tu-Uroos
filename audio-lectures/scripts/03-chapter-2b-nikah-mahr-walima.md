# Lecture 3 — Chapter 2, Part 2: The Nikah, Gold, Mahr, the Wedding Night and the Walima

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 3, the second part of Chapter 2. We cover the nikah ceremony itself, pages 147 to 163; gold for women, pages 164 to 173; the mahr, pages 174 to 185; the wedding night and the etiquette of intimacy, pages 186 to 208; and the walima, pages 209 to 223.

# Section 7: The nikah and related issues — pages 147 to 163

## The pillars

The wali's permission is required for the bride, not for the groom. He does not need to be physically present. He can be represented, or give written consent.

The bride's consent is required. If she is forced, she has the right to have the marriage annulled by a court.

Witnesses: at least two upright Muslim witnesses. "No nikah without a wali and two upright witnesses." Umar rejected a nikah witnessed by one man and one woman, calling it a secret marriage. Tirmidhi says the Companions and their students agreed there is no nikah without witnesses. Some of Malik's followers accepted a public announcement as enough, and Ahmad and Ishaq accepted one man and two women.

Offer and acceptance: the wali or his representative makes the offer, and the groom accepts aloud in front of the witnesses, saying "I accept". Signing the nikah form also shows consent, but saying "qabul" aloud is best. A mute person may consent by gesture.

## The khutbah and the ceremony — pages 151 to 153

The nikah begins with the Khutbah al-Hajah, the sermon of need: praising Allah, seeking His help and forgiveness, seeking refuge from the evil of ourselves, the shahadah, and then three verses: Al Imran 102, An-Nisa 1, and Al-Ahzab 70 to 71.

Then the officiant says, in substance: "I marry so-and-so, daughter of so-and-so, with the permission of her wali and with her consent, to so-and-so, son of so-and-so, before these witnesses, for this mahr." Then he asks the groom, "Do you accept?" and he says "I accept". The bride does not need to say the offer and acceptance herself at the ceremony; her wali acts for her. Write the names and mahr on paper beforehand to avoid mistakes. Giving advice to the couple is recommended.

The author warns that making the bride and groom recite the kalimahs at the nikah is a bid'ah, with no basis.

The du'a for the couple is: "Barakallahu laka, wa baraka alayka, wa jama'a baynakuma fi khayr." May Allah bless you, shower blessings on you, and bring you together in goodness.

## Other issues

Nikah by telephone, page 154: valid if the wali and the groom do the offer and acceptance while two upright witnesses hear it. It is better for everyone to be in one sitting, but if that is hard, for example when abroad, it is allowed with care to avoid fraud.

A woman cannot conduct a nikah, pages 154 to 155. "A woman may not marry off another woman, nor herself." Aisha arranged a marriage for her nephew, but had a man perform the contract.

The place, pages 155 to 157: there is no fixed place. The hadith "do the nikah in the mosques" is weak. The mosque is fine, and saves the cost of halls and hotels, but it is not required. A hotel is allowed if Islamic limits are kept.

The daff and songs, pages 157 to 159: young girls may sing decent songs, without shirk, obscenity or arousal, and beat the daff, which is a one-sided frame drum without jingles. The dhol and other instruments are not allowed, and the daff is not for men. "The difference between halal and haram is the daff and the voice." Aisha was told, "The Ansar like entertainment." But using this to justify music bands is wrong.

Gifts, page 159: "Give gifts to one another, and you will love one another." Umm Sulaym sent a dish to the Prophet's wedding to Zaynab, and Khadijah gave her daughter Zaynab a necklace at her wedding.

Food at the nikah, page 161: the girl's family may feed guests as hospitality. It is neither bid'ah nor required. But the author condemns huge baraat crowds descending on the bride's family. The middle way is a few close people and simple food.

Adornment, pages 162 to 163: beauty within limits is encouraged. Borrowing clothes or jewellery for a wedding is allowed; Aisha borrowed Asma's necklace. Men's perfume should have a noticeable scent but no colour, and women's should have colour and a faint scent. Gold and silk are lawful for women and forbidden for men.

# Section 8: Gold for the bride — pages 164 to 173

Women have worn gold throughout Islamic history. But Shaykh Nasir al-Din al-Albani held that ring-shaped, encircling gold, such as rings, bangles and necklaces, is haram for women, and only non-encircling gold is allowed. The author respects al-Albani but sees this as one of his isolated views. He presents Shaykh Ibn Baz's fatwa refuting it.

Ibn Baz's answer is that all gold, encircling or not, is lawful for women. His evidence:

The Qur'an, in Az-Zukhruf verse 18, describes being "brought up in ornaments" as a trait of women, without restriction.

Ali reported that the Prophet held silk in one hand and gold in the other and said: "These two are haram for the males of my Ummah," and in Ibn Majah, "lawful for its females". Abu Musa reported: "Gold and silk are lawful for the women of my Ummah and forbidden to its men."

Many scholars reported consensus. Al-Jassas, al-Kiya al-Harrasi, al-Bayhaqi, al-Nawawi and Ibn Hajar all stated that women may wear gold of every kind: rings, necklaces, bangles and anklets, and that the prohibition of the gold ring is only for men.

And there are specific hadiths. A woman came with her daughter wearing two thick gold bangles, and the Prophet asked only whether she paid zakat on them; he did not object to her wearing them. The Negus sent jewellery including a gold ring, and the Prophet gave it to his granddaughter Umamah, saying "adorn yourself with this". And Umm Salamah wore gold, and was told that if she paid its zakat, it was not hoarded treasure.

So the reports that seem to forbid it are either irregular or abrogated. Al-Albani's reconciliation fails, because the permitting reports themselves include rings and bangles. The conclusion: gold of every kind is lawful for women and forbidden for men.

# Section 9: The mahr — pages 174 to 185

Islam gave women the right to mahr: money or property the husband must give at marriage according to his means. Only the wife can waive it, fully or partly, and only willingly.

## Compensation or gift — pages 174 to 175

Some jurists see the mahr as compensation for conjugal enjoyment; others see it as a free gift. The author prefers the gift and honour view. For example, mahr is due even without consummation, and both spouses enjoy intimacy equally.

## Fixing the mahr — pages 176 to 178

From Al-Baqarah verses 236 and 237, five rules. One, the nikah is valid even if the mahr is not specified. Two, if he divorces her before consummation and no mahr was fixed, no mahr is due, but he must give her a gift according to his means. Three, if he divorces before consummation and a mahr was fixed, half of it is due, unless she waives it, and his giving all of it is closer to taqwa. Four, if divorce comes after valid seclusion, the full mahr is due. Five, if he divorces after intimacy and no mahr was fixed, she gets the customary mahr of women of her family, called mahr al-mithl.

A widow gets the full mahr in all cases, consummated or not, based on the Prophet's judgment in the case of Birwa' bint Washiq.

## When to pay, and how much — pages 178 to 182

The timing is flexible: at the nikah, before or after the rukhsati, or later. If the husband dies without paying, it is paid from his estate. The best is at the nikah or soon after.

There is no fixed amount. It depends on the husband's means. A large mahr is permitted. An-Nisa verse 20 speaks of a "heap of treasure". There is the story of Umar telling people not to exceed four hundred dirhams; a woman of Quraysh recited this verse, and Umar said, "A woman was right and Umar was wrong," and withdrew it. The Prophet's wives received five hundred dirhams. The book converts this to roughly sixty four thousand rupees by gold, or twenty to twenty five thousand by silver, in 2003 values.

There is also no minimum. The Prophet told a poor man, "Look for something, even an iron ring," and in the end married him for the Qur'an he knew. The hadith that the mahr may not be less than ten dirhams is weak.

And less is better. "The best nikah is the easiest one," and "the best mahr is the easiest".

## The myth of the "thirty two rupee Shar'i mahr" — pages 183 to 185

In Pakistan people spend lakhs on customs, but set the mahr at the so-called "Shar'i mahr" of thirty two rupees and eight annas. This comes from the Hanafi minimum of ten dirhams, converted into rupees long ago. The author shows four problems. The ten dirham hadith is weak. Ten dirhams today is worth far more. Ten dirhams was a minimum, not a standard. And if one wanted to follow the Sunnah amount, the Prophet's mahr would be around sixty thousand rupees, which is not obligatory either. The mahr should match the husband's means and mutual agreement.

Wrong practices include: fixing a mahr he never intends to pay; pressuring the wife to waive it; setting a huge mahr as a weapon against divorce; families keeping the mahr themselves; and high demands that keep daughters unmarried. The mahr belongs to the woman, and the wali cannot take it.

# Section 10: The wedding night and the etiquette of intimacy — pages 186 to 208

This section is frank, as the book is a guide for married couples. I will summarise it respectfully.

## The first meeting — pages 186 to 189

The bride is anxious entering a new home, so the groom and household should welcome her and put her at ease. At Aisha's wedding, the Prophet brought milk, drank, and offered it to her.

On the first meeting, the groom places his hand on her forehead, says Bismillah, and asks Allah for her good and the good she was created with, and seeks refuge from any evil. The author advises explaining this to the bride beforehand, so the wording does not hurt her.

It is recommended to pray two rak'ahs together on the first night. Ibn Mas'ud advised a man who feared his young bride might dislike him: love is from Allah and aversion is from Shaytan, so pray two rak'ahs with her behind you, and ask Allah to bless you in each other.

The du'a before intimacy is: "Bismillah. Allahumma jannibna al-shaytan, wa jannib al-shaytan ma razaqtana." If a child is decreed, Shaytan will not harm it.

## Consideration and medical advice — pages 189 to 193

The husband should not rush to fulfil only his own need, but should ensure his wife's satisfaction too, with affection and gentleness. The book quotes a doctor who warns that ignorance on the wedding night can cause trauma and fights. Couples should not follow crude advice from friends, and there is no pressure to complete everything on the first night. Go gently over days, attend to hygiene, and see a doctor if a problem persists.

## The lawful and the forbidden — pages 193 to 208

Any position is allowed, but intercourse only in the natural place. Anal intercourse is haram. Al-Baqarah verse 223, "your wives are a tilth for you, so approach as you wish", refers to positions, not to that. "Cursed is the one who approaches a woman in her rear."

Spouses may see each other's whole body. There is no authentic prohibition.

A wife should not refuse her husband without a valid reason, and there are strong warnings about this in Bukhari and Muslim.

Intimacy with one's wife is a charity that earns reward.

Revealing bedroom secrets is haram. "Among the worst people before Allah on the Day of Resurrection is a man who is intimate with his wife, and she with him, and then he spreads her secret." The author warns husbands against describing their wives to friends, and women against gossiping about their wedding nights.

Making wudu before repeating intimacy is recommended, and so is wudu before sleeping in a state of janabah. Bathing together is allowed; Aisha and the Prophet bathed from one vessel. The book describes the Sunnah ghusl: wash the hands, wash the private parts, make full wudu, run wet fingers through the hair roots, pour three handfuls on the head, then wash the whole body. A woman need not undo her braids for ghusl after intimacy, but should for ghusl after menses or postnatal bleeding.

Intercourse during menses and postnatal bleeding is haram, by Al-Baqarah verse 222. Everything else short of intercourse is allowed. If the wedding date falls during the bride's period, everything except intercourse is allowed. Postnatal bleeding lasts at most forty days, and if it stops earlier, intercourse is allowed. The expiation for intercourse during menses is to give one dinar, or half a dinar, in charity.

Coitus interruptus, called 'azl, which includes modern barrier methods, is permissible but disliked without need, and not disliked when there is a genuine need such as a medical one. The Companions practised it while the Qur'an was being revealed, but the Prophet also called it "the hidden burial", and said any soul destined to exist will exist.

Intercourse during pregnancy and breastfeeding is allowed.

# Section 11: The walima — pages 209 to 223

The walima is the meal given by the groom after the marriage. The meal given by the bride's family is not called walima.

## Its ruling — pages 209 to 210

Some scholars say obligatory, and the majority say recommended. The author's view is that it is obligatory, for five reasons. The Prophet commanded Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, "Give a walima, even with one sheep." He gave a walima at all his own marriages. There was no marriage in his time without one. It is not a burden, because even a glass of sherbet is enough. And it publicly announces the marriage.

## Timing, length and food — pages 211 to 213

Closest to the Sunnah is after consummation, as with the morning after the Prophet's marriage to Zaynab. Delaying a day or two for a need is fine. One day is required; more is allowed if affordable, without showing off.

Meat is best if affordable, but no one should go into debt for lavish food. Safiyyah's walima was dates and a simple barley dish. On the way back from Khaybar, the Prophet asked everyone to bring what they had, and a leather mat was spread with dates, ghee and cheese. That was the walima. The wealthy should help a poor groom.

## Whom to invite, and accepting — pages 214 to 218

Invite rich and poor, and preferably the righteous. "The worst food is the food of a walima to which the rich are invited and the poor are left out."

Accepting a walima invitation is obligatory, even when fasting. If fasting, make du'a for them; if not, eat. "Whoever refuses an invitation has disobeyed Allah and His Messenger."

But there are times not to attend: where there is music, dancing or filming, unless you can correct it; where alcohol is served or the host's income is haram; where pictures of living beings are displayed; feasts held out of pride and showing off; and when you have a valid excuse such as illness, rain or no transport.

## Etiquette for guests — pages 218 to 223

Bring a gift if you can. Make du'a for the couple, and for the host: "Allahumma barik lahum fima razaqtahum, waghfir lahum, warhamhum."

On eating and drinking standing at weddings, there are hadiths prohibiting it and hadiths showing the Prophet drank standing. The majority reconcile them: it is permissible, and the prohibition means it is disliked, not haram. The author's view: sitting is better, but standing is allowed if seating is not possible.

## Summary

To sum up: a valid nikah needs the wali, the bride's consent, two witnesses, offer and acceptance, and mahr, and it should be simple. Gold of every kind is lawful for women. The mahr is the woman's right, has no minimum or maximum, and easier is better. The wedding night has its du'as and its etiquette, with clear limits of halal and haram. And the walima is, in the author's view, obligatory, simple, and open to rich and poor alike. In the next lecture, we turn to wedding customs and dowry.
