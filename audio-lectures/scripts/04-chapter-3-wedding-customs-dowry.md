# Lecture 4 — Chapter 3: Wedding Customs and Islam, and the Question of Dowry

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 4, on Chapter 3, pages 227 to 282. The author's central point is simple: Islam made marriage easy, but we have made it costly and hard through customs. That delays marriages and fuels immorality. Some families even "plan" a child's wedding from birth. The chapter has three sections: wedding customs, sins committed at weddings, and a long, balanced review of dowry, or jahez.

# Section 1: Wedding customs — pages 227 to 241

The author goes through the customs one by one. Here they are.

Engagement customs: large parties, sweets and gifts to every relative, the couple exchanging rings themselves although they are still non-mahram, and videos. All of it is excess.

Mayun: secluding the bride in a corner for days before the wedding. It is a Hindu custom, with no basis.

Tel and mehndi: processions carrying henna from house to house with music and dancing are un-Islamic. Henna for the bride is fine, and even recommended. But for the groom, as adornment, it is not appropriate.

The sehra, the groom's head garland: said to come from Iranian fire worshippers, with a hint of shirk.

Garlands of currency notes on the groom are wasteful and risky. Even flower garlands as a ritual are a bad custom.

Salami, money given to the bride or groom when greeting them, is a petty form of neota.

The baraat: large crowds descending on the bride's home. It is a Hindu custom, against Islam's simplicity. A few people accompanying the groom is fine.

The band and music with the baraat are sinful. The author cites Surah Luqman verse 6 on "idle talk", which Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn Abbas explained as singing and music.

A decorated horse or car for the groom is imitation of non-Muslims and showing off.

Fireworks are burning money, dangerous, and forbidden extravagance.

Hijras, dancers and singers bring sin and indecency.

Throwing money over the groom is superstition, and not allowed.

Making the bride and groom recite the kalimahs at the nikah has no basis. It implies they were not Muslim before, and it is a bid'ah.

Badd, the sweets and dry fruits distributed by the groom's party, and vari, the bridal clothes and jewellery displayed to everyone, are show-off customs.

Neota, or nyondra, page 234: recording cash gifts at the walima so that they can be repaid later, often doubled. The author calls it mean and a cause of disputes, citing Surah Al-Muddaththir: "Do not give in order to gain more." A voluntary gift is fine, if it is not recorded and not expected back.

Pranks on the groom, like salty milk and stealing his shoes for ransom by the bride's sisters and other non-mahram girls, are immoral. The sarbala, the boy who accompanies the groom, is a custom of Hindu origin.

Sending the bride off under a Qur'an held over her head is superstition. The blessing of the Qur'an is in acting on it. Oil on the doorframe and the kangna wrist thread are customs of ignorance.

Visiting shrines with the couple for blessings is shirk. Good and harm come only from Allah.

Muh dikhai, unveiling the bride's face for gifts, including in front of non-mahram men, is wrong. God bharai, putting a baby boy in the bride's lap for fertility, is superstition. Chauthi, the bride returning to her parents on the fourth day, is a needless custom that depends on the husband's permission.

## "Marriage to the Qur'an" in Sindh — pages 238 to 241

The book includes a report from Takbeer magazine, 1995. Some landlords and pirs in Sindh, to keep land and status within the family, "marry" their daughters to the Qur'an. The girl declares that she gives up her right to marry. She is revered as a "bibi", but lives and dies unmarried. This leads to hysteria, immorality, and even killings. There is the story of a dying landlord begging forgiveness from his "bibi" daughter, who refused him. The author calls it a grave injustice committed in the name of the Qur'an.

# Section 2: Sins committed at weddings — pages 242 to 247

Shaving the beard is haram. It changes Allah's creation, contradicts the command to let the beard grow and trim the moustache, and imitates disbelievers and women.

Plucking eyebrows: Ibn Mas'ud reported that Allah cursed women who tattoo, pluck their eyebrows, and file their teeth for beauty, changing Allah's creation.

Long nails are against the fitrah. Nails, the moustache, and armpit and pubic hair should be trimmed within forty days at most.

A gold ring for the groom is haram, because gold is forbidden for men.

Photography and filming at weddings, of the couple and of guests, including non-mahram women, and showing the videos to others, is immodesty. Footage of women dancing at weddings has even ended up sold in the market. It is not allowed.

Tight and sheer clothes, and unveiling: "Women who are clothed yet naked will not enter Paradise, nor smell its fragrance."

Mixed gatherings: "The zina of the eyes is looking, of the ears is listening, of the tongue is speaking, of the hand is touching, of the feet is walking; the heart desires, and the private part confirms it or denies it." If a woman passing men while wearing perfume is condemned, how much more a fully adorned bride among men.

# Section 3: Dowry, or jahez — pages 248 to 282

This is one of the most important discussions in the book. The author says jahez came to Muslims from long coexistence with Hindus, from the Hindu practice of kanyadan. There are two extremes. Some treat dowry as a binding part of marriage. Others call any jahez haram and a curse. The author takes the middle path.

## The meaning — page 249

In Arabic, jahaz means preparing provisions, as in Surah Yusuf, and "whoever equips a fighter has fought". In custom, it means the goods sent from the bride's side to the groom's home. Greed for jahez is now so strong that girls without it cannot marry.

## The Shar'i ruling — pages 250 to 254

Demanding jahez, or making it a condition of marriage from either side, is forbidden and invalid, for three reasons.

One, neither the Prophet nor the Companions ever had such a condition. The Sunnah is our model, and "any condition not in Allah's Book is void, even if it is a hundred conditions".

Two, all household costs and maintenance are the husband's duty, by An-Nisa verse 34, not the bride's family's. Voluntary help or gifts from a wealthy wife or in-laws are allowed.

Three, it replaces a daughter's inheritance. Hindu society denied daughters inheritance and gave dowry instead. Muslims copy this, even though Allah fixed her share in An-Nisa verses 11 to 14.

## The harms — pages 255 to 267

Religious harms: it is a bid'ah crushing a Sunnah; it leads to choosing brides for wealth rather than religion; and it deprives daughters of their inheritance.

Social harms, eight of them. Poor parents are oppressed, and start saving from the day a girl is born. Dowry is displayed to show off, which is riya, called the minor shirk. Parents resort to bribes, lies and interest to pay for it. Girls are left unmarried because parents cannot afford it. Daughters are seen as a burden, just like the pre-Islamic attitude in An-Nahl verses 58 and 59, and this even leads to female foeticide. Some girls commit suicide because their parents cannot afford a dowry. Brides are abused, or even burnt in so-called "stove burst" deaths, for "insufficient" dowry. And there is hypocrisy, when people condemn dowry when marrying their daughters but demand it for their sons.

Begging for dowry, page 263: people beg in mosques for their daughters' dowry. But begging is permitted for only three: one who took on a debt to reconcile people, one struck by a calamity, and one in severe poverty attested by three wise men. Otherwise it is eating haram.

Medical and moral harms: delaying marriage brings illness, and delaying daughters' marriages for dowry leads to zina and the spread of indecency.

The author summarises: jahez is not part of the Shari'ah; treating it as a condition is wrong; demanding it is un-Islamic and immoral; denying inheritance in its place violates Allah's limits; it is a Hindu custom; it did not exist in this form in the Prophet's time; and its small financial benefit does not outweigh its countless harms. He notes that even several Indian states passed laws against dowry, yet the custom persists.

## Doubts answered — pages 268 to 277

Is jahez a curse, a la'nat? The author says calling it la'nat needs proof, and there is no text cursing it. But its current form is a Hindu custom, and "whoever imitates a people is one of them". If it is not a demand or a condition, there is some allowance, with conditions.

Is jahez the root of all problems? No. That is overstated. It is one social problem among many.

Did the Prophet give dowry to his daughters? He had four daughters: Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum and Fatimah. There is no authentic report of dowry for Ruqayyah or Umm Kulthum, who both married Uthman, and Uthman was wealthy. For Zaynab, the Prophet gave no dowry, but Khadijah gave her a necklace as a gift. That shows parents may give gifts at marriage, not as a custom or for show.

What about Fatimah? Nasa'i has a chapter on a man equipping his daughter: the Prophet equipped Fatimah with a velvet sheet, a water skin and a pillow stuffed with grass. But Ali had no mahr, and the Prophet told him to give his armour. Ali sold it, and the Prophet had Bilal buy perfume and household items with that money. So the items were most likely bought from Ali's own mahr money. And even if they came from the Prophet himself, Ali was his ward, raised in his care, and very poor, and helping such a person is praiseworthy. There is also the story of Fatimah asking for a servant, and the Prophet teaching her instead to say subhanallah thirty three times, alhamdulillah thirty three times and Allahu akbar thirty four times before sleep.

The conclusions: the mahr may be demanded in advance, but jahez may not be made a condition. The mahr can be in goods instead of cash. Helping a poor son-in-law with essentials is good. Gifts at weddings are recommended, but not to show off, and they do not cancel a daughter's inheritance. Demanding jahez is shameful.

The case of Umm Habibah, pages 278 to 280: after her husband became a Christian in Abyssinia, the Prophet sent a messenger to the Negus, who married her to the Prophet with a mahr of four thousand dirhams, and provided her jahez himself. The lesson: giving goods at marriage was known, and the Prophet's silence implies some permissibility. But it was nothing like today's custom. Accepting unrequested gifts is fine; demanding them is not.

## The author's twenty suggestions — pages 280 to 282

He ends with practical guidance. Jahez is not Shar'i and not a condition. It must not replace inheritance. Demanding it is a crime, and the government may restrict and penalise it. Treating it as a necessary part of marriage makes it a bid'ah. But calling it absolutely haram is also extreme.

Buying household goods from the mahr money is fine. Helping a poor son-in-law is fine. Parents may give gifts out of happiness. But no display, no debt, no begging or illicit means, and do not exceed your means.

It is better to give gifts after the wedding, at a suitable time, so as not to resemble the Hindu custom. And prefer cash over goods. Cash ends the display, lets the couple buy what they actually need, avoids extravagance, and can even help start a business.

The groom's side should work even harder to end this custom. And in everything, moderation, not extremes.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 3: almost every popular wedding custom, from mayun and sehra to the baraat, band, fireworks and neota, is either of Hindu origin, wasteful, superstitious or sinful. Shaving the beard, plucking eyebrows, filming and mixed gatherings are sins committed at weddings. And jahez, as practised today, is not Islamic: demanding it is forbidden and it must never replace a daughter's inheritance, though voluntary, modest gifts are allowed.
