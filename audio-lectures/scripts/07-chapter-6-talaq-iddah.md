# Lecture 7 — Chapter 6: Talaq, or Divorce, and Iddah

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 7, on Chapter 6, pages 395 to 430. It covers the status and correct method of talaq, talaq under compulsion or intoxication, written and phone talaq, three talaqs in one sitting, divorcing at the parents' command, the wife of a missing husband, the rules of iddah, and mourning.

A reminder: this lecture explains what the book says. It is not a fatwa. If you face a real situation of divorce or iddah, please confirm with a qualified scholar.

# Section 1: The status and method of talaq — page 397 onwards

## Divorce is discouraged

Marriage is a solemn covenant. Talaq and khul' exist only for when living together becomes impossible. Laqit ibn Sabirah complained to the Prophet, peace be upon him, about his wife's sharp tongue. The Prophet said divorce her. He said they had a child and a long companionship. The Prophet said: "Then advise her. If there is good in her, she will listen."

The author contrasts this with Hindu law, which has no divorce at all. Islam allows an exit for valid reasons, but dislikes it without reason. "Any woman who asks for divorce without a reason, the fragrance of Paradise is forbidden to her." And: "The greatest sin with Allah is a man who marries a woman, fulfils his need, then divorces her and takes her mahr."

## The Sunnah method — page 399

Not in haste. After reflection, before two upright witnesses, give one talaq, while she is in a state of purity in which they have not had intercourse, or while she is pregnant. Talaq during menses, or in a purity in which they had intercourse, is not the Sunnah way; it is called bid'ah talaq.

Ibn Umar divorced his wife during her menses. The Prophet told Umar: "Order him to take her back, and then divorce her when she is pure or pregnant, if he wishes."

Witnesses are commanded in Surah At-Talaq, but the talaq takes effect even without them; having them is a precaution. Talaq happens with clear words, and also with indirect words if divorce was intended. So the author advises avoiding such words when angry.

## During the iddah — pages 400 to 402

After a first or second talaq, which is revocable, the wife stays in her husband's house during the iddah, and he provides housing and maintenance. Surah At-Talaq says do not expel them. At the end, either keep her kindly or part kindly, with two witnesses. The wisdom is that it gives a chance for reconciliation. In Pakistan, the wife is often sent to her parents immediately, which is contrary to the Qur'an.

After the third talaq, there is no housing or maintenance, as the Prophet told Fatimah bint Qays, unless she is pregnant, in which case he maintains her until delivery.

## Return, and the second and third talaq — pages 402 to 404

After the first talaq, during the iddah, he may take her back, which is called ruju', without a new nikah. After the iddah ends, they may remarry with a new nikah, and the guardians must not prevent it.

The second talaq follows the same procedure. Hafiz Abd al-Mannan Nurpuri explains that the verse "talaq is twice" covers this, with no fixed gap required, but two or three at once count as one.

After a third talaq, she becomes unlawful for him until she genuinely marries another man, not for halala, and he divorces her or dies. Then the first husband may remarry her if both agree.

## Written and phone talaq — pages 404 to 405

Talaq takes effect whether spoken or written, face to face or by phone, and whether or not she receives the letter. Even if said jokingly. But a mere thought, without speaking or writing it, does not count: "Allah has forgiven my Ummah what crosses their minds, as long as they do not act on it or speak it."

## Under compulsion, intoxication or insanity — pages 405 to 406

If relatives or others force a husband by violence or threats to divorce, it does not take effect. "There is no talaq under ighlaq," and scholars explained ighlaq as compulsion. "Allah has pardoned my Ummah for mistakes, forgetfulness, and what they are forced into." Ibn Abbas said the talaq of the drunk and the coerced is not valid. Ibn Qudamah lists Umar, Ali, Ibn Umar, Ibn Abbas and many others as holding that coerced talaq is invalid.

## Three talaqs in one sitting — pages 406 to 409

This is a key issue, and the author's view is that three talaqs said or written at once count as one revocable talaq. His evidence:

One, Ibn Abbas reported in Muslim that in the time of the Prophet, of Abu Bakr, and the first two years of Umar's caliphate, three talaqs were counted as one. Then Umar said: "People are hastening in a matter in which they had latitude. Let us enforce it on them," and he did. The author explains this was an administrative, punitive measure by Umar, not a change in the Shari'ah, and reports that Umar later regretted it, citing Ibn al-Qayyim.

Two, Rukanah ibn Abd Yazid divorced his wife three times in one sitting and grieved over it. The Prophet asked: "In one sitting?" He said yes. The Prophet said: "That is only one. Take her back if you wish." And he did.

The wisdom: before Islam, men divorced and took back their wives endlessly. Islam limited it to three, with time to reflect between. Counting three at once as three is unjust, because angry men often say it many times and regret it later. Those who count them as three push people toward halala. Following the view closer to the Sunnah avoids halala and saves homes.

# Section 2: Divorcing the wife at the parents' command — pages 410 to 417

The principle is: "There is no obedience to any creature in disobedience to the Creator," and "obedience is only in what is right." The rule the author gives: if the parents' demand is based on a reasonable cause, such as the wife's bad conduct or religion, the son should comply. If it is based on whim or spite, he need not obey, and they should be advised against it.

Two stories. Ibrahim visited Isma'il's home when Isma'il was away. The first wife complained of hardship and did not host him, so he left the message "change your doorstep", and Isma'il divorced her. The second wife praised Allah and hosted him, and he said "keep your doorstep". This shows the father's advice was based on a valid reason.

And Umar disliked a wife that his son Abdullah ibn Umar loved. The Prophet told Abdullah, "Obey your father." The author says Umar had a valid reason, and scholars note that obeying parents takes precedence over desire when their command agrees with religion.

Mulla Ali Qari held that a son is not obliged to divorce at his parents' order even if they are hurt. The author calls this the other extreme. He agrees with Ibn al-Arabi and al-Mundhiri: if the demand rests on a reasonable cause, obey; otherwise it is not obligatory.

# Section 3: The wife of a missing husband — pages 418 to 422

There is no explicit Qur'an or hadith text, so the Companions' rulings are followed. She waits four years, then observes the widow's iddah of four months and ten days, and then she may remarry. This was ruled by Umar, Uthman, Ibn Abbas, Ibn Umar and others, and most say the four years start from when the case reaches the court.

Imam Bukhari's view was to wait one year, by analogy with lost property. The author says the Companions' view is generally stronger, but where the woman faces hardship, in her chastity or maintenance, a court ruling after one year may be allowed. The Hanafi view, waiting until the husband would be ninety or a hundred and twenty years old, is impractical, and modern Hanafis like Ashraf Ali Thanawi give fatwa on the Maliki four years out of necessity.

If the first husband returns after she has remarried, scholars differ. Ibn Hajar reports that he may choose either to take her back or to take back his mahr. The author's view is that the first husband retains the right.

# Section 4: The rules of iddah — pages 423 to 428

Iddah is the waiting period after separation. No engagement or marriage is allowed during it. The periods are:

One, a divorced woman who menstruates: three menstrual cycles.

Two, a woman who does not menstruate, because of old age or young age: three months.

Three, a pregnant divorcee: until she gives birth.

Four, a widow: four months and ten days.

Five, the wife of a missing husband: four years of waiting, then four months and ten days.

Six, khul': one menstrual cycle, or one month if she does not menstruate.

Seven, a woman divorced before consummation or valid seclusion has no iddah at all. But if the husband dies before consummation, she observes four months and ten days.

Eight, a slave woman: two cycles.

Nine, if the husband gives a second talaq during a revocable iddah, a new iddah starts. If he dies during a revocable iddah, she switches to the widow's iddah.

Ten, if during the iddah she turns out to be pregnant, the iddah lasts until delivery.

# Section 5: Mourning — pages 426 to 427

A widow mourns during her iddah. She avoids attractive clothes, jewellery, adornment, henna, kohl and perfume. "It is not lawful for a believing woman to mourn for anyone more than three days, except for her husband, four months and ten days."

She should stay in her husband's house for the iddah, going out only for essential needs by day and returning by night. Furay'ah bint Malik wanted to move to her family after her husband was killed, and the Prophet told her, "Stay in your house until the term is complete." Divorced women likewise. But if the house is unsafe for her honour or life, she may move to relatives.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 6: divorce is disliked, and should be a last resort. The Sunnah method is one talaq, in a state of purity without intercourse, before witnesses, with the wife staying at home during the iddah. Talaq under compulsion or intoxication does not count, and in the author's view three talaqs in one sitting count as one. A son should obey his parents' demand for divorce only if it rests on a reasonable cause. The wife of a missing husband waits four years. And each kind of separation has its own iddah, with the widow observing mourning for four months and ten days. In the next lecture, we look at complex marriage issues.
