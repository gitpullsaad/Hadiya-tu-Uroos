# Lecture 0 — Introduction to the Book and the Series

Assalamu alaykum, and welcome to this series of audio lectures on the book Hadiya tul Uroos, which means "The Gift for the Bride". It was written by Hafiz Mubashir Hussain Lahori, and published by Mubashir Academy in Lahore in June 2003. It is a book of about six hundred pages, in Urdu, and its subtitle is "the rulings and issues of married life, from the Qur'an and Sunnah".

Before we begin, a word about these lectures. They are based entirely on a set of chapter by chapter study notes made from the book. The notes are summaries in plain words, not the author's exact text. So when you hear "the author says", it means the notes report this as the author's position. Whenever I mention a page number, it is the book's printed page number. If you open the PDF, add two to find the PDF page.

These lectures explain what the book teaches. They are not a fatwa. For any personal situation, especially divorce, iddah or inheritance, please confirm with a qualified scholar.

## The preface — pages 5 to 6

The author explains why he wrote the book. Years earlier, he and some friends planned a campaign against un-Islamic wedding customs in Pakistan. At that time he wrote two books, one on dowry and one on wedding customs, but they were not published then.

He noticed a gap. There was no Urdu book that covered marriage and family issues directly from the Qur'an and Sunnah and was also suited to Pakistani society. He criticises an earlier book, Tuhfat ul Uroos, which is an Urdu translation of an Arabic work. In his view it does not match the Pakistani context, and he disagrees with its position that ring-shaped gold jewellery is forbidden for women. We will come back to that gold question in Lecture 3.

His aim was a complete book, covering everything from choosing a spouse to the walima, then the rights of spouses, of the newborn and of in-laws, then divorce, iddah and much more. He also included fatwas of Arab scholars translated into Urdu. He intended it as a wedding gift, and as a kind of encyclopedia of marital and women's issues.

## The map of the book

The book has twelve chapters. Here is the map, so you know where each lecture fits.

Chapter 1 is about the need for marriage, and the harms of abandoning it. That is Lecture 1.

Chapter 2 is the longest. It is the Islamic way of marriage, from choosing a spouse all the way to the walima. Because it is so long, it is split into two lectures. Lecture 2 covers choosing a spouse, seeing the bride, marriage bureaus, engagement, the wali, and love marriage. Lecture 3 covers the nikah itself, gold for women, mahr, the wedding night, and the walima.

Chapter 3 is wedding customs and Islam, including a long and balanced discussion of dowry, or jahez. That is Lecture 4.

Chapter 4 is the rights and duties of husband and wife, the rights of the newborn child, and the relationship between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law. That is Lecture 5.

Chapter 5 is forbidden and invalid marriages: mut'ah, halala and shighar. That is Lecture 6.

Chapter 6 is talaq, or divorce, and iddah. That is Lecture 7.

Chapter 7 is complex marriage issues, such as child marriage and the marriage of those who have committed zina. That is Lecture 8.

Chapter 8 is zihar, ila and li'an. That is Lecture 9.

Chapter 9 is special issues for spouses and for women, mostly fatwas of Arab scholars, and hijab. That is Lecture 10.

Chapter 10 is polygamy. That is Lecture 11.

Chapter 11 is Islam and birth control. That is Lecture 12.

Chapter 12 is an appendix of household tips and home remedies, compiled by the author's wife. That is Lecture 13.

Finally, Lecture 14 brings everything together. It is a summary of the whole book's teaching, the total knowledge, in one place.

## The author's method

A few features of the author's method run through the whole book, and it helps to notice them from the start.

First, he argues from the Qur'an and the hadith, and he names his sources, mainly Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, Nasa'i, Ibn Majah and Ahmad. He often comments on whether a hadith is authentic or weak, frequently relying on Shaykh al-Albani's grading.

Second, he presents different scholarly opinions, and then gives his own preferred view. In these lectures I will try to make clear which view is whose, and which one the author prefers.

Third, he writes for Pakistani society. Again and again he criticises customs that came from Hindu culture, such as the baraat, dowry demands and denying daughters their inheritance, and he calls Muslims back to the simplicity of the Sunnah.

Fourth, he takes a middle path. On dowry, on polygamy, on the joint family system, and on obeying parents in divorce, he rejects both extremes.

With that, let us begin with Chapter 1, in the next lecture.
