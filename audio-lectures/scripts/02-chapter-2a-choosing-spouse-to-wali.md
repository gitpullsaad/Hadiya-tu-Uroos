# Lecture 2 — Chapter 2, Part 1: Choosing a Spouse, Engagement, the Wali and Love Marriage

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 2. Chapter 2 of the book, pages 73 to 221, is called "The Islamic Way of Marriage: From Choosing a Spouse to Walima". It is the longest chapter, so we cover it in two parts. In this part we look at whom you may and may not marry, how to choose a spouse, seeing the prospective bride, marriage bureaus, engagement, the role of the wali or guardian, and love marriage and court marriage.

# Section 1: Whom you may marry — pages 75 to 86

Islam forbids some relationships permanently and some temporarily. Marriage with all non-Muslims is forbidden, with one exception: Muslim men may marry chaste women of the People of the Book, that is Jews and Christians.

## Marriage with non-Muslims — pages 76 to 78

Surah Al-Baqarah verse 221 says: do not marry polytheist women until they believe, and a believing slave woman is better. And do not marry your women to polytheist men. The reason given is that they invite to the Fire, and a spouse has a deep influence on one's faith and on the children. Surah Al-Mumtahanah verse 10 says that believing women are not lawful for disbelievers, nor the reverse.

If a spouse becomes an apostate, the marriage is void. And if someone holds beliefs of kufr or shirk while calling himself a Muslim, marriage with him is not valid.

## Women of the Book — pages 78 to 79

Surah Al-Ma'idah verse 5 makes chaste women of the People of the Book lawful, with mahr, in marriage and not in fornication. The author gives five conditions. One, only Muslim men may do this. A Muslim woman may never marry a Jewish or Christian man. Two, she must be chaste. Three, it must be a proper nikah, with witnesses, the wali's consent, offer and acceptance, and mahr. Four, she must truly be a Jew or Christian who believes in Allah, revelation and the Hereafter, not an atheist in name only. Five, if it endangers his faith or his children, it is better avoided.

## Permanently forbidden women — pages 82 to 85

By lineage: mother and grandmothers, daughters and granddaughters, sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, and the daughters of one's brother and sister.

By breastfeeding: "What is forbidden by lineage is forbidden by suckling." The woman who nursed a child becomes his mother, her husband becomes his father, and their relatives become mahram for that child only, not for the child's siblings. There are two conditions. The nursing must be within the first two years, and there must be at least five separate full feedings. One feeding is counted when the child leaves the breast by its own choice.

By marriage: the wife's mother and grandmothers; the wife's daughter, if the marriage with her mother was consummated; the father's wife; and the son's wife.

And by li'an: after li'an, the couple can never remarry. We will explain li'an in Lecture 9.

## Temporarily forbidden women — pages 85 to 86

These are forbidden only while a certain situation lasts. One, two sisters together. Two, a woman together with her paternal or maternal aunt. Three, a married woman, until she is widowed or divorced and completes her iddah. Four, any woman during her iddah. Five, a woman divorced three times, for the same husband, until she genuinely marries another man, not for halala, and that marriage ends. Six, a woman known for zina, unless she sincerely repents.

# Section 2: Priorities in choosing a spouse — pages 87 to 97

## Religion first — pages 87 to 89

Choose a spouse for religion and taqwa, because spouses deeply influence each other and the children. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: "A woman is married for four things: her wealth, her lineage, her beauty and her religion. Choose the religious one, may your hands be dusted." That is in Bukhari and Muslim.

And in Tirmidhi: "If someone whose religion and character please you comes to propose, marry your daughter to him. Otherwise there will be fitnah and great corruption on earth."

Also: "The world is provision, and the best provision of this world is a righteous wife." That is in Muslim.

## Three stories — pages 90 to 94

First, Musa at Madyan, in Surah Al-Qasas. The father, most commentators say it was Shu'ayb, married his daughter to Musa, a poor stranger, because he was strong and trustworthy. The mahr was eight to ten years of service.

Second, Umm Sulaym and Abu Talhah. She refused him while he was a disbeliever, and her mahr was his acceptance of Islam. The author draws a lesson against marrying non-Muslims in the hope that they will convert.

Third, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib. He refused the Caliph Abd al-Malik's proposal for his heir, and instead married his learned and beautiful daughter to his poor student, whose wife had just died, for a mahr of two or three dirhams. He brought her to the student's house himself that same evening.

## Other priorities alongside religion — pages 94 to 97

One, a virgin. "Marry virgins, for they have sweeter mouths, more fertile wombs, and are content with little." But Jabir married a widow because he needed someone to look after his young sisters, and the Prophet accepted his reason. So marrying a widow or divorcee for a good reason is fine.

Two, a loving and fertile woman.

Three, beauty together with good character. Allah is beautiful and loves beauty. And the best woman is one who pleases her husband when he looks at her, obeys him when he asks, and does not oppose him regarding herself or his wealth in ways he dislikes.

# Section 3: Seeing the prospective bride — pages 98 to 102

Looking at a non-mahram woman is not allowed, but looking at a woman one intends to marry is allowed, and even recommended. The Prophet told Mughirah ibn Shu'bah: "Look at her, for it is more likely to create lasting love between you." Her parents hesitated, but the girl herself said, if the Prophet ordered it, then look. Jabir even hid to see a girl he wished to marry. Imam Ahmad permitted it even without her knowledge, if the intention is marriage.

The author's view: refusing to let the man see the girl causes problems later, and can lead to divorce. Better a quiet refusal before marriage than a broken marriage after. He criticises families who let their daughters go out unveiled, yet object to a proposed groom seeing her once.

The limits: the stronger view is that only the face and hands are seen. The girl may also see the boy. But there must be no private meetings.

# Section 4: Marriage bureaus — "killers of honour" — page 103 onwards

This section uses a report from the monthly magazine Tayyibat, Lahore, 2002. Daughters are a blessing, but dowry and greed make parents anxious, and marriage bureaus exploit that anxiety. They work as businesses, give false praise of proposals, know nothing real about the family or character, and charge fees.

The case studies are painful. A sister married through a bureau to a supposed airline employee, who took her to his village, where she disappeared. A bureau husband who sold girls into marriages abroad. A girl whose bureau photos were misused for blackmail and whose engagement was broken. Internet sites publishing girls' photos. And a young man who married through a website a woman much older than claimed, already divorced with children, which ended in divorce.

The author's conclusion: trust Allah, make du'a, use the Prophet's criterion of religion first, and drop the artificial standards of caste, wealth and customs that keep good girls unmarried.

# Section 5: Engagement — pages 112 to 118

Is engagement allowed? Yes. It is a pre-agreement before nikah. But it is only permissible, not a required part of marriage. In the Prophet's time people usually went straight to the nikah, sometimes with a delayed rukhsati. Engagement is useful when the families need time to know each other.

Engagement customs, such as processions, sweets and gift exchanges, are wasteful. The engagement ring: men may wear only silver, and women gold or silver. Giving a ring as a gift is fine, but treating the ring as a ritual symbol of engagement is a custom of Christian origin that should be dropped, as al-Albani said in Adab al-Zifaf.

Proposing over another's proposal is forbidden, until the first man withdraws or gives permission. Some scholars allow it if the first proposal has not yet been accepted, based on the case of Fatimah bint Qays, who received three proposals at once and was advised by the Prophet to marry Usamah. And if the first proposer is irreligious, a righteous person may propose.

The engaged couple: seeing each other once, or talking in the presence of others, is fine. But private meetings, outings and being alone are not allowed, because they are still non-mahram. "No man should be alone with a woman except with a mahram." Phone calls and letters are also better avoided.

Breaking an engagement: it is a promise, and keeping it is a moral duty, but it may be broken for a valid reason. Better to break it than to marry and divorce. And breaking an engagement is not a talaq. Ending a nikah, however, does require a talaq, even before the rukhsati.

# Section 6: The wali, or guardian — pages 119 to 137

## Who is the wali — page 119

The wali is the nearest male relative: the father, then the paternal grandfather, then the brother, then the paternal uncle, and so on. A grown son can sometimes be the wali of his widowed or divorced mother. If there is no wali, the ruler is the wali: "The sultan is the wali of the one who has no wali." The wali's consent is required for every woman, adult or minor, virgin or previously married.

## The Qur'anic evidence — pages 120 to 124

One, in Al-Baqarah verse 221, Allah addresses men: do not marry your women to polytheists. So the marriage of women is handled through their guardians. Qurtubi called it a text that there is no nikah without a wali.

Two, Al-Baqarah verse 232: "Do not prevent them from marrying their husbands." It was revealed about Ma'qil ibn Yasar, who refused to let his sister remarry her former husband. Ibn Kathir and many others said this proves a woman cannot marry herself off, because otherwise the wali's refusal would not matter.

Three, An-Nur verse 32, "marry off the single among you", is addressed to guardians.

## The hadith evidence — page 124 onwards

Aisha described the types of marriage before Islam. The one that remained was a man proposing to the girl's wali, giving mahr, and marrying her. Islam abolished the rest.

"There is no nikah without a wali." This is in Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah, and graded sahih. Hakim lists around thirty Companions who narrated it.

And Aisha's narration: "Any woman who marries without her wali's permission, her nikah is void, void, void." If the marriage was consummated, she gets the mahr, and if they dispute, the sultan is the wali of the one who has none.

## The jurists — pages 126 to 128

Malik, Shafi'i, Ahmad, Hasan al-Basri and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i all held that the wali is a condition, and that a nikah without him is void.

The author's conclusion: from the Qur'an, the hadith and the majority of scholars, the stronger view is that a woman's nikah, whether virgin or previously married, is invalid without her wali's permission.

The Hanafi view is different. An adult woman's nikah without a wali is valid, but if the husband is not her equal in standing, called kuf', it can be annulled. The author says the clear evidence favours the majority.

## A joint statement of scholars — pages 129 to 132

The book reproduces a joint statement from 1996, titled "No sane adult Muslim girl may contract her own nikah without the consent of her family or wali". It summarises the schools of thought, favours a balance requiring both the girl's and the family's consent, and criticises Western individualism. It was signed by scholars across all the schools: Deobandi, Barelvi, Ahl-e-Hadith, Shia and Jamaat-e-Islami.

## Why a wali for girls — pages 133 to 134

Three reasons. Men handle outside affairs and can investigate a suitor better. A woman's natural modesty makes it hard for her to propose for herself or bear rejection, so the wali protects her. And such a weighty decision benefits from the guardians' experience.

## The girl's consent is also required — pages 134 to 137

The wali cannot force her. Her consent is a condition, just like his. A forced marriage can be annulled by a court. "A previously married woman is not married until she is consulted, and a virgin until her permission is sought, and her permission is her silence." Khansa bint Khidham was married by her father against her will, and the Prophet annulled it. And a girl married to her cousin to raise his status was given the choice. She said: "I accept what my father did, but I wanted women to know that fathers have no say in this on their own."

# Love marriage and court marriage — pages 138 to 144

The permissible form: if "love marriage" means the boy and girl both like the match, and it is done openly through the families, with the wali, witnesses and mahr, then it is not only allowed, Islam likes it. Allah says "marry whom you like". The wali should respect the girl's wish when the match is suitable, and must not obstruct it without reason, as in the case of Ma'qil ibn Yasar.

The impermissible form: a secret affair, then elopement, then a court marriage without the wali. Islam considers this void, because there is no nikah without a wali. Islam also forbids the steps that lead there: being alone together, meetings and phone affairs. The author says parents share the blame, through lack of Islamic upbringing, television and films, and co-education.

The fate of girls who run away is described through a quotation from Maulana Naeem Siddiqui. Many are abandoned once the "love" fades, their families are disgraced, and their sisters' marriages are harmed. Some lovers delay the nikah and leave her when she is pregnant. Some turn out to be already married or poor. Some girls end up sold or in brothels, and many commit suicide. A few return to their parents, and a small number get their parents to accept the match after a child is born. Siddiqui estimates no more than about five percent end successfully.

## Summary

To sum up this part: choose a spouse for religion first. Know who is permanently and who is temporarily forbidden. Seeing the prospective bride is Sunnah, within limits. Beware of marriage bureaus. Engagement is allowed but not required, and the engaged couple are still strangers to each other. And a woman's nikah requires both the wali's consent and her own consent.
