# Lecture 6 — Chapter 5: Forbidden, Corrupt and Void Marriages

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 6, on Chapter 5, pages 377 to 396. The chapter deals with three kinds of marriage that Islam forbids: mut'ah, or temporary marriage; halala, the "marriage" done to make a thrice-divorced woman lawful for her first husband again; and shighar, the exchange marriage known in Pakistan as watta satta.

# Section 1: Mut'ah, or temporary marriage — pages 379 to 381

Mut'ah is a marriage for a fixed period, hours or days, in return for payment, which ends automatically when the time is up. It was allowed early in Islam in limited circumstances, during travel or war, and then permanently prohibited at the time of the conquest of Makkah. Some Companions did not hear of the prohibition until Umar enforced it.

The evidence for its prohibition:

One, Surah Al-Mu'minun verses 5 and 6 say that intimacy is lawful only with wives or slave women. A mut'ah woman is neither, because she has no inheritance, no maintenance, no divorce and no iddah. Ibn Abbas said this verse abrogated mut'ah. Some claim that Ibn Abbas allowed it, but the author notes that he retracted.

Two, Ali reported that the Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade mut'ah, and the meat of domestic donkeys, at Khaybar. That is in Bukhari and Muslim.

Three, Sabrah al-Juhani reported that the Prophet said: "I had permitted mut'ah, but Allah has now forbidden it until the Day of Resurrection. Whoever has such a woman, let her go, and do not take back what you gave her." That is in Muslim.

Four, Jabir said it was practised in Abu Bakr's time and early in Umar's, until Umar forbade it.

Five, Umar said the Prophet permitted it three times and then forbade it.

Six, Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq said about mut'ah: "It is zina itself."

# Section 2: Halala — pages 382 to 392

After three talaqs, given in separate sittings, the wife becomes unlawful for her husband, unless she genuinely marries another man who later divorces her or dies. That is Al-Baqarah verse 230. Halala is when a man marries her with the intention or condition of divorcing her after one night, so that she becomes lawful for her first husband again. It is cursed and haram.

The evidence is very strong. Ibn Mas'ud reported: "The Messenger of Allah cursed the muhallil and the one for whom it is done." The muhallil is the man who does the halala. Similar reports come from Jabir, Ali and Ibn Abbas. Uqbah ibn Amir reported that the Prophet asked: "Shall I tell you about the borrowed billy-goat?" They said yes. He said: "It is the muhallil. Allah has cursed the muhallil and the one for whom it is done."

Ibn Umar was asked about a man who married a woman to make her lawful for his brother. He said: "No. Only a marriage of genuine desire. In the time of the Prophet we considered this to be fornication." Uthman separated such a couple. Umar said that any muhallil brought to him, he would stone. Ibn Umar also said that such people remain fornicators even if they stay together for twenty years, if Allah knows they intended halala. Ibn Abbas said: "Whoever tries to deceive Allah, Allah will deceive him." And Hasan al-Basri and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i said that if any one of the three, the husband, the wife or the muhallil, intended halala, the nikah is invalid and does not make her lawful.

Some jurists said halala is sinful but still makes her lawful for the first husband, and today some people even offer halala as a "service". The author says the clear texts make it haram, and all such tricks must be avoided.

## How to avoid halala — pages 386 to 387

Why does the need for halala arise so often? Because many people count three talaqs pronounced in one sitting as three. The author, following the Ahl-e-Hadith view, says that by the Qur'an and Sunnah, three talaqs in one sitting count as one revocable talaq. If people follow this, halala is never needed. The detailed evidence is in Chapter 6, which we cover in the next lecture.

# Section 3: Shighar, or exchange marriage — pages 388 to 393

Shighar means marrying off one's daughter or sister to someone on the condition that he marries his daughter or sister to you. Ibn Umar reported in Bukhari and Muslim that the Prophet forbade shighar, which is a man marrying his daughter to another on condition that the other marries him his daughter, with no mahr between them.

## The dispute

Scholars disagree. Is "no mahr" an essential part of the definition, or incidental? And is that definition from the Prophet himself, or from a narrator? All jurists agree that an exchange with no mahr is haram. They differ when a mahr is fixed. Malik and Ibn Hazm say it is still void. The Shafi'is, Hanafis and Hanbalis say it is valid if a proper customary mahr is fixed.

## The author's view

The author's preferred view is that shighar is prohibited whether or not a mahr is fixed, because its essence is the condition of exchange.

His strongest proof: Abbas ibn Abdullah married his daughter to Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Hakam, who married his own daughter to him, with mahr fixed. Mu'awiyah ordered Marwan to separate them, saying: "This is the shighar that the Prophet forbade." No Companion objected. Tirmidhi also reports that shighar is annulled even with mahr, which was the view of Shafi'i, Ahmad and Ishaq.

The author explains that the "no mahr" definition is most likely from Nafi', the narrator, and Bukhari has Nafi' explaining it. Even Imam Shafi'i was unsure whether it came from the Prophet, Ibn Umar, Nafi' or Malik.

Other hadiths: Abu Hurayrah reported that shighar is "marry me your daughter and I will marry you mine, or your sister and I will marry you mine". And: "There is no shighar in Islam."

## The exception — page 393

If there is no condition, and two families independently and freely marry into each other, it is allowed. The book cites a fatwa of Hafiz Abd al-Mannan Nurpuri: a man's daughter was married into a family, and three years later that family's daughter was proposed for his son, without any prior condition. That is not shighar.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 5: mut'ah was permitted briefly and then forbidden until the Day of Resurrection. Halala is cursed, and the author says it can be avoided entirely by counting three talaqs in one sitting as one. And shighar, exchange marriage with a condition, is forbidden in the author's view even if mahr is fixed, though two families freely marrying into each other without any condition is fine.
