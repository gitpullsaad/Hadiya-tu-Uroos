# Lecture 1 — Chapter 1: The Need for Marriage, and the Harms of Abandoning It

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 1, on Chapter 1 of Hadiya tul Uroos, pages 23 to 74. The chapter answers three questions. Why do human beings need marriage? What happens when people avoid it? And what obstacles stop people from marrying, and how can they be removed?

# Section 1: The need and importance of marriage

The author describes five kinds of need, and then the virtue of marriage.

## One: a natural need — pages 25 to 27

Islam is the religion of fitrah, of human nature, and marriage meets a natural need. It is allowed for everyone, and for some adults who have the means, it can even become obligatory.

The evidence includes Surah An-Nisa, chapter 4 verse 3: marry two, three or four, but if you fear you cannot be just, then only one. And the famous hadith in Bukhari and Muslim: "O young men, whoever among you can afford it should marry, for it lowers the gaze and guards chastity. And whoever cannot, should fast, for fasting is a shield for him."

The author gives an analogy. Sexual desire is like hunger, and marriage is its proper food. Other outlets, such as masturbation, zina and homosexuality, are forbidden. He cites Surah Al-Isra, "do not even approach zina", and Surah An-Nur on the punishment of one hundred lashes for the unmarried fornicator, while the married adulterer is stoned, as reported in Bukhari.

There is also the hadith of Abu Dharr in Muslim. Intimacy with one's own wife is a charity that earns reward. If done in haram it would be a sin, so when done in halal, it earns reward.

## Two: a social need — pages 28 to 31

The human race has continued since Adam and Hawwa through marriage. The verses cited include An-Nisa verse 1, An-Nahl verse 72, and Al-Baqarah verses 223 and 187.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: "Marry the loving, child-bearing woman, for I will boast of your great numbers." In the report of Ma'qil ibn Yasar, a man asked about a beautiful, noble woman who could not have children. The Prophet said no, three times.

## Three: a moral need — pages 32 to 34

Suppressing desire completely is unnatural, but releasing it without control corrupts society. Islam channels it through marriage. Surah An-Nur verse 32 says: "Marry off the single among you."

There are three people whom Allah has taken it upon Himself to help: the one striving in Allah's path, the slave paying for his freedom, and the one who marries seeking chastity. That is in Tirmidhi, Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.

And in the hadith of Jabir in Muslim, the Prophet said that if a man is attracted by a woman he sees, let him go to his own wife, for she has the same as what the other woman has.

## Four: a spiritual and psychological need — page 35

Spouses are a source of tranquillity for each other. Surah Ar-Rum verse 21 speaks of the love and mercy Allah has placed between husband and wife, and Al-A'raf verse 189 says the spouse was created so that one may find rest in her.

## Five: a religious need — pages 36 to 37

Marriage is the Sunnah of the Prophet. He said: "Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me." That is in Bukhari and Muslim. The wording "Nikah is my Sunnah" is in Ibn Majah, but the author notes it is weak.

There is also the report in Tabarani: "When a servant marries, he has completed half of his religion, so let him fear Allah in the other half." Al-Albani graded it hasan because of supporting chains.

## The virtue of marriage — pages 38 to 39

The poor Companions complained that the rich could give charity. The Prophet told them that glorifying Allah, enjoining good, and even intimacy with one's wife, are all charity. Spending on one's family, even a morsel placed in the wife's mouth, is charity.

Ibn Abbas told Sa'id ibn Jubayr: "Marry, for the best of this Ummah had the most wives." The author adds a balancing comment. Having more wives is not automatically better, and the Qur'an and Sunnah set the limit at four.

# Section 2: Ways of avoiding marriage, and their consequences — pages 40 to 63

The author describes three kinds of people who avoid marriage.

## First: Sufism, monasticism and celibacy — pages 40 to 44

Some people think that staying single gives more time for worship. The author says this is a Christian monastic idea, and that it led to immorality in the monasteries, citing Maududi's Tafhim ul Qur'an. Hinduism and Buddhism have similar ideas.

The Prophet forbade celibacy. Uthman ibn Maz'un asked permission to remain celibate and was refused. Companions who asked to castrate themselves were forbidden. And there is the hadith of the three men: one wanted to pray all night, one to fast every day, and one to stay away from women. The Prophet said: "I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me."

The harms of abandoning marriage are three. First, many medical illnesses. Second, desire pushes people to illicit means, causing moral corruption. Ibn al-Jawzi, in Talbis Iblis, wrote about Sufis who abandoned marriage and fell into sin. Third, social harm, because it stops the continuation of the human race, against Allah's design.

## Second: avoiding marriage because zina is easy — pages 45 to 57

Some people avoid the responsibilities of marriage, such as maintenance, raising children and providing a home, and satisfy their desire through prostitution. They even mock marriage, asking why keep a buffalo when milk is sold in the market. The author says this mindset comes from Western ideas of "freedom" now spreading in Muslim lands.

He traces the roots in the West, mostly drawing on Maududi's book Purdah. One, the idea of freedom of thought after the French Revolution, which saw morality as a barrier to progress. Two, Malthus in 1798 and the Neo-Malthusians, who feared population would outgrow resources and promoted birth control. Contraceptives removed the last natural barrier, the fear of pregnancy, to illicit relations. Three, industrialists who pulled women into factories and offices, and clubs, cinemas and dance halls that exploited desire. Four, the misuse of inventions such as radio, television, the VCR and the computer, which brought obscenity into homes. Five, co-education and the mixing of the sexes.

He then lists ten consequences of this permissiveness: the spread of prostitution; a sexualised environment harming children; mass sale of contraceptives; venereal diseases; relationships without marriage, mistresses, "trial marriages" and homosexuality; the breakdown of the family and disputes over lineage and inheritance; the abortion business; the killing of the maternal instinct, and even of children; the plight of elderly parents left in old age homes; and the end of respect for women.

He also notes that some in the West have reconsidered, for example an American magazine that warned of a "satanic trinity" of obscene literature, films and women's degrading dress, which brought down civilisations like Rome.

## Third: abandoning marriage for lack of means — pages 58 to 63

Unlike the first two, this can be a genuine excuse. If a man is physically unable, through impotence or illness, he is not to blame. But if such a man marries anyway, his wife can seek khul'. If he is financially unable, the Shari'ah allows him to delay until he is able, but it requires him to stay chaste. Surah An-Nur verse 33 says: "Let those who cannot marry keep chaste until Allah enriches them."

The author gives seven ways to stay chaste:

One, fast frequently, as in the "O young men" hadith.

Two, avoid anything that arouses desire: obscene literature, songs, films and dramas.

Three, lower the gaze from non-mahrams, as in An-Nur verses 30 and 31. The Prophet told Ali: "Do not follow one glance with another. The first is forgiven, but not the second."

Four, keep righteous company. "A man is upon the religion of his friend." And the parable of the perfume seller and the blacksmith.

Five, follow medical advice from a qualified doctor.

Six, avoid idleness and solitude.

Seven, taqwa and fear of Allah, as in the example of Prophet Yusuf, who refused the wife of al-Aziz.

# Section 3: Obstacles to marriage, and their solutions — pages 64 to 74

## Education — page 64 onwards

Many parents delay their children's marriages "until education is complete". The author separates two cases. If there are genuinely no finances during study, delay is acceptable. But if the means exist, delaying merely out of fear for studies is wrong. Marriage actually frees the mind from sexual distraction and helps concentration. He says he knows many who married while studying and did better.

He quotes Shaykh Abdullah Nasih Ulwan, who as a student at Al-Azhar thought marriage would hinder him. After marrying, he found stability, and his focus improved.

## Expenses — pages 67 to 69

Marriage is often delayed because of inflated "required" expenses: a separate house, all the wedding customs, lights, huge guest lists, the baraat, lavish meals, gifts to in-laws, and on the girl's side heavy dowry, jewellery and feeding the baraat. Most of these are unnecessary.

The model is the marriage of Ali and Fatimah. Ali was too shy to ask. The Prophet asked whether he had anything for the mahr. He had only his armour, and that became the mahr. Some reports say it bought a bed, a pillow, a hand mill and two water skins. There was no baraat, no dowry procession, and no customs.

The best mahr is the easiest. In the Prophet's time some marriages had a mahr of a pair of shoes, a handful of dates, or teaching some surahs of the Qur'an. And the walima can be small, even just sherbet or milk.

## Shyness — page 70

It is the parents' duty to arrange the marriage of their grown children, gently persuading a shy child and considering the child's preference. If parents neglect it, the son or daughter may raise it without shame, because it is their right, preferably through a close relative such as an aunt or uncle.

## Age — pages 70 to 72

In Pakistan, marrying before about twenty to twenty two for girls, or twenty five to thirty for boys, is considered odd. But Islam encourages marrying soon after puberty. The benefits of early marriage are: protection from sin at the peak of desire; enjoying love and tranquillity in youth; early responsibility keeps young people away from wasteful habits; medically, delay causes problems; and your children grow up to support you before old age, instead of you raising young children when you are old.

## Summary of Chapter 1

To sum up: marriage is a natural, social, moral, psychological and religious need, and it is the Sunnah. Celibacy for worship is rejected. Avoiding marriage for easy zina is condemned, and the author traces it to Western permissiveness. Lack of means is a valid reason to delay, but only with chastity. And the obstacles of education, expense, shyness and age should be removed by returning to the simplicity of the Sunnah.
