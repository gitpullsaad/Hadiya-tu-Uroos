# Lecture 5 — Chapter 4: The Rights of Husband and Wife, the Newborn, and In-laws

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 5, on Chapter 4, pages 283 to 378. It has four sections: the rights of the husband, the rights of the wife, the rights of the newborn child including naming, aqiqah and upbringing, and finally the rights and duties of mother-in-law and daughter-in-law.

# Section 1: The rights of the husband — pages 285 to 298

The husband's right is very great. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said: "If I were to order anyone to prostrate to another, I would order a wife to prostrate to her husband."

## Conjugal right

When he calls her for his need, she should come, "even if she is at the oven". If she refuses without a reason, the angels curse her until morning.

## Obedience

Men are the protectors and maintainers, qawwam, by An-Nisa verse 34. "If a woman prays her five, fasts Ramadan, guards her chastity and obeys her husband, she may enter Paradise by whichever gate she wishes." And to one woman the Prophet said about her husband: "He is your Paradise or your Hell."

## Housework — pages 288 to 290

The wife should run the home: cooking, cleaning and so on. Fatimah's hands blistered from the hand mill, and when she asked for a servant, she was given tasbih instead. Asma bint Abi Bakr cared for her husband Zubayr's horse, and carried date stones on her head for two miles. The reason is that the husband bears all expenses, so this division of roles is fair.

## Guarding his property and her honour — pages 290 to 293

She guards his property in his absence. She should not spend from his house without permission, and giving charity from his wealth needs his general or specific permission. She guards her honour, does not soften her speech with men, and does not let anyone he dislikes enter his home.

## Leaving home, fasting, gratitude — pages 291 to 296

She should not leave home without his permission, by Al-Ahzab verse 33. Ibn Taymiyyah said doing so makes her disobedient.

She should not keep voluntary fasts without his permission when he is present. There is the case of Safwan ibn al-Mu'attal, whose wife complained about him, and the Prophet ruled that a wife should not fast voluntarily without her husband's permission.

She should be grateful to him. The Prophet told women to give charity, because he saw most of the people of Hell were women, as they cursed much and were ungrateful to their husbands.

## Disciplining a disobedient wife — pages 296 to 297

An-Nisa verse 34 gives three steps, in order: first advise her, second separate beds, and third light, non-injurious hitting, only after the first two fail. "If they obey you, seek no way against them." In the Farewell Sermon he said, if they commit clear wrong, hit lightly, not causing injury. And he said: "Let none of you whip his wife like a slave and then sleep with her at the end of the day." Unprovoked hitting is not allowed.

## Other rights

The husband has the right of talaq if the wife's rebellion becomes intolerable, done in the Sunnah way. He may marry up to four with justice. And he inherits half of his wife's estate if she has no children, or a quarter if she has children.

# Section 2: The rights of the wife — page 299 onwards

Al-Baqarah verse 228 says women have rights similar to those against them. In the Farewell Sermon: "Treat women well. You have rights over them, and they have rights over you." And the Prophet told Abdullah ibn Amr, who fasted every day and prayed all night: "Your body has a right over you, your eyes have a right over you, and your wife has a right over you."

## Her conjugal right — pages 300 to 302

Abdullah ibn Amr's wife said he never came to bed because of his worship. The Prophet told him to reduce his fasting and Qur'an recitation, because "your wife has a right over you". And Salman told Abu al-Darda', who spent all his time in worship: "Your Lord has a right over you, your self has a right, and your family has a right. Give each its right." The Prophet said: "Salman spoke the truth."

## Going abroad after marriage — pages 302 to 303

Many men work abroad for years. Umar ruled that soldiers should not be away from their wives for more than four months, or by some reports six. Longer than that only for a genuine need. If it causes hardship or fitnah, the wife may seek khul'.

## Housing and maintenance — pages 303 to 305

The husband provides housing according to his means. Co-wives get separate dwellings, as the Prophet gave each wife her own room. All expenses are on the husband. "Feed her when you eat, clothe her when you clothe yourself, do not strike the face, do not revile her." If the husband is miserly, the wife may take what is sufficient for herself and the children, according to custom, without his knowledge, as in the case of Hind and Abu Sufyan.

The mahr is her right, and no one may seize it or pressure her to waive it.

## Good treatment — pages 306 to 307

"Live with them in kindness." "No believing man should hate a believing woman; if he dislikes one trait in her, he will be pleased with another." "The best of you is the best to his family, and I am the best of you to my family." And woman was created from a rib; if you try to straighten it you will break it, so enjoy her as she is.

## Justice between wives — pages 307 to 309

If you fear injustice, then only one. "Whoever has two wives and inclines to one will come on the Day of Resurrection with half his body leaning." Equal spending, housing and turns. A new wife who is a virgin gets seven nights, and a previously married woman gets three; then the turns are divided equally.

## Guiding her, and protecting her honour — pages 309 to 310

"Save yourselves and your families from the Fire." "Each of you is a shepherd." And the dayyuth, a man without protective jealousy who tolerates indecency in his family, will not enter Paradise.

## The right of khul' — pages 310 to 312

If the wife has a reasonable aversion to her husband, she can ask for separation by returning the mahr. If he refuses, she goes to court. The wife of Thabit ibn Qays said: "I do not fault his religion or character, but I fear ingratitude in Islam." The Prophet asked: "Will you return his garden?" She said yes, and he told Thabit to take the garden and separate from her. Her iddah is one menstrual cycle.

Is khul' a talaq or an annulment? The author's stronger view is that it is an annulment, faskh. There is no right of return; it is the woman's initiative; she may remarry the same husband with a new nikah; and the iddah is one period. But he also warns: "Any woman who asks her husband for a divorce without a reason, the fragrance of Paradise is forbidden to her."

The wife inherits a quarter of her husband's estate if he has no children, and an eighth if he has children.

# Section 3: The rights of the newborn — page 314 onwards

## Rejoicing at a boy or a girl — pages 314 to 316

Being sad at a girl's birth, or even divorcing the wife for it, is wrong. Allah gives sons, daughters, both, or none, as He wills. The pre-Islamic Arabs grieved at daughters. And we do not know which is better: the wife of Imran hoped for a son, but was given Maryam, the mother of Isa.

On infertility: it is often due to the man, not the woman, so seek medical treatment. Avoid amulets, which are shirk; ruqyah from righteous people is fine. A man who wants children may marry a second wife, but divorcing the first is wrong.

## The adhan in the newborn's ear — pages 317 to 326

This has been practised for centuries. Recently Mufti Mubashir Ahmad Rabbani argued it is not Sunnah, because the main report, from Abu Rafi', has a weak narrator, and other reports are fabricated. A response argued that the narrator is weak but not abandoned, that the report is strengthened by support, that Tirmidhi said "the practice is upon it", and that many scholars, including Nawawi, Ibn Qudamah and Ibn al-Qayyim, considered it Sunnah. Ibn al-Qayyim explained the wisdom: the first words the child hears are the greatness of Allah and tawhid.

The author's preferred view, page 324: giving the adhan is permissible and Sunnah, and the safer position, because of the centuries of the Ummah's practice.

## Tahneek and circumcision — page 325

Tahneek is Sunnah: chew a date and put a little in the baby's mouth, with du'a. Circumcision of boys should be done as early as possible; it is one of the five acts of fitrah.

## Naming — pages 326 to 339

Some reports show naming on the day of birth, and others on the seventh day with the aqiqah. The reconciliation: if no aqiqah is planned, name on the first day; otherwise the seventh, or any time within seven days.

Choose names with good meanings. Change bad names, as the Prophet renamed Umar's daughter Asiyah, meaning "disobedient", to Jamilah. Prefer names of prophets and righteous people. Names implying shirk are forbidden, such as Nabi Bakhsh, Pir Bakhsh, Abd al-Nabi, Ghulam Nabi and Abd al-Rasul, and so are names based on numerology. The most beloved names to Allah are Abdullah and Abd al-Rahman.

The book then gives long lists of names with their meanings: names of prophets, names describing the Prophet, the names of his wives, children and grandchildren, and around a hundred and fifty names of male Companions and a hundred and thirty of female Companions.

## Aqiqah — pages 340 to 344

Some scholars said obligatory, the majority said emphasised Sunnah, and the Hanafis said merely permissible. The author's view: whoever can afford it must do it. Two animals for a boy and one for a girl. If only one is affordable for a boy, one is enough. If unable, do it later when able.

The animal is a goat or sheep, male or female. A cow or camel is not established; Aisha refused a camel and insisted on two sheep as the Prophet said. Sharing the aqiqah in a qurbani cow is against the Sunnah. The time is the seventh day, otherwise the fourteenth or twenty first, or whenever able.

## Upbringing — pages 344 to 355

Parents are responsible for their children's education and upbringing until adulthood, and for their marriage, treating sons and daughters equally. "Whoever raises two girls until maturity will come on the Day of Resurrection like this with me," and he joined his fingers. A poor woman split her only date between her two daughters, and the Prophet said Allah had made Paradise obligatory for her.

The book includes an article on raising children, from Muhammad Jamil Zainu. Its main points:

Faith: teach the kalimah and its meaning early, love of Allah, hope for Paradise, and fear of Hell. "When you ask, ask Allah."

Prayer and Qur'an: "Teach your children prayer at seven, discipline them for it at ten, and separate their beds." Pray in front of them, take them to the mosque, teach Al-Fatihah and short surahs, and correct mistakes gently.

Avoiding haram: stop swearing and cursing; explain the harms of gambling and smoking; keep them from obscene material and films; teach truthfulness, with no "joking lies"; feed them only halal; never curse your children, but say "may Allah reform you"; and warn them against shirk.

Covering: girls should wear modest clothes from childhood, the headscarf from about seven, and full hijab at puberty. Boys and girls must not imitate the opposite sex.

Manners: use the right hand, say Bismillah and Alhamdulillah, eat sitting, keep clean, correct them privately and gently, separate boys' and girls' beds, greet with salam, be kind to neighbours, and honour guests.

# Section 4: Mother-in-law and daughter-in-law — pages 356 to 378

This section is adapted mainly from Muhammad Abdul Hafeez's book Saas aur Bahu. In the past the mother-in-law dominated; now, increasingly, the daughter-in-law dominates. Islam offers the middle way: love, sacrifice, forgiveness, respect for elders and kindness to the young.

## Our predicament — pages 357 to 358

Muslims of the subcontinent kept Hindu customs such as the joint family and dowry. The typical cycle: the bride is courted lavishly during the engagement, then criticised after the wedding. Small matters snowball into jealousy and insults. The son is caught between his mother and his wife, and a demand for a separate house follows.

## Three solutions — pages 359 to 363

One, seek Allah's pleasure. Answer anger with love, harshness with gentleness, and evil with good.

Two, want for others what you want for yourself. "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself."

Three, the joint family system. The author says it is a custom, not a religious requirement. A woman has a right to her own home where she is in charge. Joint living causes problems: no privacy for the couple, hijab from brothers-in-law becomes impossible, and the wife must please many people. People say they cannot afford a separate house, yet spend lakhs on wedding customs, while the Prophet's houses were single rooms. He gave each wife a separate dwelling, and gave Ali and Fatimah their own house. But the real aim is not to promote separate living. It is to apply Islamic etiquette, and each family can choose what works.

## The duties of each person — pages 364 to 376

The bride's parents should give their daughter a religious upbringing, teach her that obeying her husband, except in sin, is obligatory, and that her in-laws are like her own parents. They should not encourage her to dominate, or let her run home with complaints. The Prophet sent Fatimah back to Ali after a complaint, and reconciled them.

The mother-in-law should accept that her son's attention will shift to his wife, treat the bride like a daughter, overlook her faults, never taunt her about dowry, stay out of the couple's private matters, protect hijab arrangements in the home, not overburden her with housework, and not speak against her to her son.

The son, as a son, must honour his parents, and should not make his wife do all the service to his parents that he should do himself. As a husband, he should give her a peaceful home, care for her in illness and childbirth, let her visit her parents, protect her from the in-law men, because "the brother-in-law is death", give her pocket money, teach her religion, and balance the rights of his parents and his wife.

The daughter-in-law should respect the mother who raised her husband, obey her husband, and help him honour his parents. She should not abandon her children to her mother-in-law's care, nor neglect the elderly. "He is not of us who does not respect our elders and show mercy to our young." She should keep good ties with in-laws even if living apart. "Whoever wants increased provision and a longer life, let him keep ties of kinship." And all should cut the waste of time and money on things like excessive shopping and entertainment, and spend it on others' rights instead.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 4: the husband's rights include intimacy, obedience in what is right, care of the home and his property, and gratitude. The wife's rights include intimacy, housing, full maintenance, her mahr, kind treatment, justice if there are co-wives, and the right of khul'. The newborn has rights of rejoicing, the adhan, tahneek, a good name, aqiqah, circumcision and a sound Islamic upbringing, with special reward for raising daughters. And between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, the cure is seeking Allah's pleasure, loving for others what you love for yourself, and each person fulfilling their own duties.
