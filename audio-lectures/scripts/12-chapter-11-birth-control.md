# Lecture 12 — Chapter 11: Islam and Birth Control

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 12, on Chapter 11, pages 547 to 577. The chapter has two sections. The first is largely taken from Maulana Sayyid Abul A'la Maududi's essay "Islam and Birth Control". It examines family planning as a national movement. The second section gives the cases in which birth control is permitted for an individual, and its limits, with fatwas from Saudi scholars.

# Section 1: Islam and family planning — page 549 onwards

## The nature of the issue

Family planning is an old idea under a new name: the fear that population grows faster than resources. Malthus argued that population grows geometrically, one, two, four, eight, while food grows only arithmetically, one, two, three, four. The old methods were infanticide, abortion and contraception. The modern ones are drugs, devices and sterilisation, called birth control or family planning.

## Is the fear correct? — pages 551 to 556

Maududi argues that history disproves it. Population never grew as Malthus predicted. And resources are not fixed: humans keep discovering new ones, such as coal, steam, oil, electricity and atomic power. Population is also a producer, not only a consumer, and need drives innovation.

He gives examples. Germany had forty five million people in 1880, and people emigrated because of hunger. By 1914 it had sixty eight million and needed foreign workers. After the Second World War, West Germany absorbed about ten million refugees and still prospered. Holland grew from about one million in the eighteenth century to ten million by 1950, on reclaimed land, and exported food. Britain grew from twelve million to forty six million between 1789 and 1913, despite emigration, and became wealthier. Worldwide, population surged after the eighteenth century, yet living standards rose dramatically.

So the correct remedy is to increase resources, not to reduce population.

## Who is the real planner? — pages 557 to 560

Maududi describes the biology. There are hundreds of millions of sperm in each emission, about four hundred thousand immature eggs in a woman, about one egg each cycle, and hundreds of opportunities over a lifetime. Only the Creator decides which union results in a child, and its sex, abilities and appearance. Despite this enormous capacity, human population had grown to only about three billion, at that time, over thousands of years. This, he says, shows a divine plan is controlling it.

## Why "family" planning rather than "population" planning? — pages 561 to 562

True population planning would require the state to control reproduction completely, abolishing marriage and the family, which no one would accept. So instead it is pushed onto individual families, through appeals to self-interest, such as a higher standard of living and children's welfare, and mass distribution of contraceptives.

## The consequences — page 562 onwards

One, population decline. When individuals put comfort before national need, numbers fall. France was the first country to adopt widespread birth control in the nineteenth century. Deaths began to exceed births, and by 1933 most of its regions had more deaths than births. A weakened France then faced defeat in the world wars. Maududi warns Pakistan, which is surrounded by large populations.

Two, moral destruction. The self-interest mindset makes people see children, elderly parents, orphaned relatives and the sick as burdens.

Three, the spread of zina. Easy contraception removes the last barrier, the fear of pregnancy, especially alongside co-education, obscene media, and legal obstacles to early marriage and polygamy.

## The individual versus the mass movement — page 566

Maududi draws an important distinction. A married couple, with a genuine personal need, using contraception on a scholar's fatwa or a doctor's advice, is a limited matter. A mass campaign distributing contraceptives to everyone is entirely different, and produces the harms just described.

## The Islamic view — pages 567 to 570

The hadiths on 'azl, withdrawal, dealt with individual questions, not a mass movement. Using them to justify a national birth control campaign is a false analogy.

And the premise of the movement, that provision will run out, contradicts the Qur'an. Provision is Allah's responsibility. "There is no creature on earth but that its provision is upon Allah," in Surah Hud verse 6. "Allah is the Provider," in Adh-Dhariyat verse 58. And Al-An'am verse 151 and Al-Isra verse 31 say: "Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you." Maududi explains that the error of the pre-Islamic Arabs was twofold: killing their children, and believing that children cause poverty. Preventing children out of that same fear repeats the second error. That is the end of Maududi's essay.

# Section 2: Permitted cases and limits — pages 571 to 575

## The principle

In principle, birth control out of fear of scarcity, whether as state policy or individual policy, is not allowed. But for a genuine reason, such as a medical one, individual use is permitted.

## The methods

The book lists four: one, the condom or the loop, the IUD; two, contraceptive drugs or injections; three, sterilisation, which is permanent; and four, abortion.

## Medical necessity

If a pregnancy would definitely harm a woman's health or life, for example after repeated caesareans when doctors advise against another pregnancy, she may use suitable contraception while the need lasts.

## Abortion for medical necessity — pages 572 to 573

Abortion is a sin. But if the mother's life is truly at risk from the pregnancy or delivery, abortion is allowed. The mother is the root, and the child the branch. Saving the certain life takes precedence over the potential life, and necessity makes the forbidden permissible. If both can be saved, doctors must try to save both. The decision should be based on expert doctors' advice.

## The analogy with 'azl

The Companions practised 'azl without necessity, and the Prophet, peace be upon him, did not forbid it, though he called it "the hidden burial". So 'azl is disliked, but not haram. Modern temporary methods like the condom or loop, for spacing children and similar reasons, may be judged by analogy. But this cannot justify a collective movement. The Saudi Permanent Committee holds the same balanced view.

## Fatwas — pages 574 to 575

The Saudi Committee said that using contraceptive pills out of fear of having many children, or of expenses, is not allowed. If pregnancy harms the woman, or she cannot deliver normally and needs surgery each time, it is allowed on the advice of a trustworthy doctor. And if the pills themselves harm her, she should avoid them.

Shaykh Ibn Baz reported that the Council of Senior Scholars ruled contraceptive pills impermissible as a general policy, because Allah legislated means to increase offspring. "Marry the loving, fertile woman, for I will boast of your numbers." The Ummah needs its numbers. It is allowed only for necessity: for example, an illness of the womb harming the woman, or many children close together harming her health. Then temporary use, for example for two years during breastfeeding, so she can recover and raise her children, is fine. But it is not allowed for a career, for comfort, to keep a job, or to keep up a standard of living.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 11: through Maududi's essay, the book argues that the fear behind family planning is mistaken, that resources grow with population, that Allah is the real planner and provider, and that a mass birth control movement leads to population decline, moral decay and the spread of zina. But for an individual couple, with a genuine medical need, temporary contraception is allowed, 'azl is disliked but not haram, and abortion is allowed only when the mother's life is truly at risk.
