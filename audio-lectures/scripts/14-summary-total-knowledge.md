# Lecture 14 — The Whole Book in One Lecture: The Total Knowledge of Hadiya tul Uroos

Assalamu alaykum. This is the final lecture. We have gone through all twelve chapters of Hadiya tul Uroos. Now let us bring the whole book together: its main lessons, the author's key positions, and the threads that run through every chapter. You can listen to this lecture on its own as a complete overview, or as a revision after the others.

# Part one: Five themes that run through the whole book

## Theme one: Marriage is a need, and it should be easy

Marriage is a natural, social, moral, psychological and religious need, and it is the Sunnah of the Prophet, peace be upon him. Celibacy for the sake of worship is rejected. Those who cannot afford to marry should fast and stay chaste. And the obstacles people put in the way, waiting until education is complete, inflated expenses, shyness and late age, should be removed. The model is the marriage of Ali and Fatimah, with armour as the mahr and no customs at all.

## Theme two: Return to the Sunnah, and leave Hindu customs

Again and again the author identifies customs that came from Hindu culture: the baraat, mayun, the sehra, dowry demands, the joint family as a religious duty, looking down on widows, and above all, denying daughters their inheritance and giving dowry instead. His call is to return to the simplicity of the Sunnah.

## Theme three: The middle path

The author rejects extremes. On dowry: demanding it is forbidden, but calling every gift a curse is also extreme. On polygamy: it is permitted, but it is Sunnah, not obligatory, and only with strict conditions. On the joint family: it is a custom, not a religious duty, and each family can choose. On divorcing at the parents' command: obey if there is a reasonable cause, but not for whim. And between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law: neither should dominate.

## Theme four: Rights are mutual

The husband has great rights: obedience in what is right, intimacy, care of the home and his property, and gratitude. But the wife also has great rights: her mahr, full maintenance, housing, kind treatment, intimacy, justice between co-wives, and the right of khul'. "The best of you is the best to his family."

## Theme five: Protecting chastity and the family

The book constantly warns against what leads to zina: mixing of men and women, being alone together, obscene media, co-education, elopement and court marriages, and the permissive ideas of the modern West. And it treats the family, lineage and the upbringing of children as things to be guarded carefully.

# Part two: The author's key positions, chapter by chapter

Here is a quick list of the most important positions, so you can remember them.

Chapter 1. If means exist, do not delay marriage for studies; marriage helps concentration. Early marriage has many benefits.

Chapter 2. Choose a spouse for religion first. Muslim men may marry chaste Jewish or Christian women, but Muslim women may never marry non-Muslims. Seeing the prospective bride is Sunnah, limited to the face and hands, with no private meetings. Engagement is allowed but not required, and the engaged couple remain non-mahram. A woman's nikah is invalid without the wali's permission, which is the author's conclusion with the majority, against the Hanafi view. But her own consent is also required, and a forced marriage can be annulled. Elopement and court marriage without the wali is void. The nikah needs two upright witnesses and a public offer and acceptance. Reciting the kalimahs at the nikah is a bid'ah. Gold of every kind, including rings and bangles, is lawful for women, following Ibn Baz against al-Albani. The mahr is a gift and honour to the wife, has no fixed minimum or maximum, and easier is better; the "thirty two rupee Shar'i mahr" is a myth. And the walima is obligatory in the author's view, given after consummation, open to rich and poor alike.

Chapter 3. Almost all popular wedding customs are of Hindu origin, wasteful or sinful. Demanding dowry is forbidden; it must not replace a daughter's inheritance; but voluntary, modest gifts are allowed, preferably in cash and after the wedding.

Chapter 4. Discipline of a disobedient wife follows three steps in order, and only light, non-injurious hitting as the last resort, never unprovoked. A husband should not be away from his wife for more than about four months without real need. Khul', in the author's view, is an annulment, not a talaq, with an iddah of one cycle. Giving the adhan in the newborn's ear is Sunnah in the author's view. Aqiqah is two animals for a boy and one for a girl, on the seventh day, for whoever can afford it. Names implying shirk are forbidden. Raising daughters well earns Paradise. And the joint family system is a custom, not a religious duty.

Chapter 5. Mut'ah is forbidden until the Day of Resurrection. Halala is cursed. Shighar, exchange marriage with a condition, is forbidden in the author's view even if mahr is fixed.

Chapter 6. Divorce is a last resort. The Sunnah method is one talaq, in a state of purity without intercourse, before witnesses, and the wife stays in the home during the iddah. Talaq under compulsion or intoxication does not count. Three talaqs in one sitting count as one revocable talaq, the author's Ahl-e-Hadith view, which also makes halala unnecessary. The wife of a missing husband waits four years. The widow's iddah is four months and ten days, with mourning, in her husband's house.

Chapter 7. The book holds child marriage permissible in the Shari'ah, with the right to annul after puberty, and criticises the Pakistani law restricting it. Those who committed zina with each other may marry after repentance, and the man responsible may marry a woman pregnant by him, with conditions. A known fornicator may not be married until they repent.

Chapter 8. Zihar does not end the marriage but needs a heavy kaffarah. "You are haram for me" is an oath, unless talaq was intended. Ila beyond four months forces a choice to return or divorce. Li'an separates the couple forever.

Chapter 9. Kissing breaks neither wudu nor the fast. A wife should not stay with a husband who has entirely abandoned prayer. A woman may recite the Qur'an from memory during menses. Plucking eyebrows and wigs are forbidden. Covering the face is required, and the husband's male relatives, "the in-law is death", are where most negligence happens.

Chapter 10. Polygamy up to four is established by Qur'an, Sunnah and consensus. But it is Sunnah, not obligatory, and requires financial means, physical ability, a purpose beyond lust, and justice. In Pakistan most men do not need it. Prefer marrying widows.

Chapter 11. The fear behind family planning is mistaken, and a mass birth control movement is not allowed. But an individual couple with a genuine medical need may use temporary contraception, 'azl is disliked but not haram, and abortion is allowed only when the mother's life is truly at risk.

Chapter 12. A practical appendix of kitchen, beauty and household tips for the new bride.

# Part three: A day in the life of the book's teaching

To make it concrete, imagine the path of a marriage as the book describes it.

A young man who can afford it decides to marry, without waiting needlessly. He chooses a girl for her religion and character. He sees her once, in the presence of her family. Her father, as her wali, agrees, and so does she. There may be a short engagement, but no ring ritual and no outings together. The nikah is held simply, perhaps in a mosque, with the Khutbah al-Hajah, two upright witnesses, a mahr that he can actually pay, and a du'a for blessing. No baraat, no band, no fireworks, no demand for dowry. On the first night they pray two rak'ahs together, and he is gentle with her. The next day he gives a walima, even a simple one, and invites rich and poor.

In their life together, each fulfils the other's rights. He provides a home and maintenance and treats her kindly; she cares for the home and is faithful. When a child is born, boy or girl, they rejoice, give the adhan, do tahneek, choose a good name, give the aqiqah, and raise the child with faith and good manners. With his parents, everyone seeks Allah's pleasure and fulfils their own duties.

If problems come, they advise, they reconcile, and only as a last resort is there one talaq in the Sunnah way, with a real chance of return during the iddah.

That is the picture the whole book is painting: a marriage that is easy, simple, just, and built on the Qur'an and Sunnah.
