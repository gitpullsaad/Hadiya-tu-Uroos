# Lecture 11 — Chapter 10: Polygamy

Assalamu alaykum. This is Lecture 11, on Chapter 10, pages 499 to 548, on polygamy, having more than one wife. The chapter has two sections. The first, adapted from an article by Professor Saeed Mujtaba Saeedi in the monthly Muhaddith, establishes that polygamy is permitted and explains its wisdom. The second is the author's own practical discussion of second marriage in Pakistani society, where he sets out strict conditions. The key message is that a second marriage is Sunnah, not obligatory, and only when its conditions are met.

# Section 1: Permissibility and wisdom — page 501 onwards

## The modern contradiction

The article notes a contradiction. The West spreads obscenity, through pictures, films and mixing, while promoting monogamy. The Encyclopaedia Britannica attributes the decline of polygamy to industrialisation, mass education and Western prestige. Some modern Muslims also call polygamy unjust or backward, and reinterpret the verses. One claimed that the Qur'an's principle is one wife, and that the permission in An-Nisa was only for wartime orphans and widows.

## History

Polygamy existed in earlier revealed laws. The article mentions, from the Bible and Muslim historians: Lamech with two wives; Ibrahim with Hajar and Sarah at the same time, and later a third wife; Ya'qub, who married the sisters Leah and Rachel, and their two handmaids; Esau, son of Ishaq; Dawud, with ninety nine wives according to one tafsir; Sulayman, of whom Bukhari reports he said he would go round ninety or more women in one night; and Musa, who married Shu'ayb's daughter and a Cushite woman. Among Hindus, kings and Krishna had many wives, and in the Mahabharata, Draupadi had five husbands.

Before Islam, Arabs had unlimited wives, and sometimes a woman had several men. Islam set a balanced limit of four.

## The Qur'an — pages 507 to 510

An-Nisa verse 3: "If you fear you will not be just to the orphans, then marry the women you like, two, three or four. But if you fear you will not be just, then only one." Aisha explained that guardians who wanted to marry orphan girls in their care without a fair mahr were told to marry other women instead.

The article rebuts Ghulam Ahmad Parwez, who claimed polygamy is allowed only in war or orphan situations. The condition about orphans concerns treating orphans unjustly, not restricting polygamy to emergencies. An-Nisa verse 129 says "you will never be able to be perfectly just between wives", but this refers to emotional inclination. The justice required is in outward treatment. Maududi and Amin Ahsan Islahi explained that a conditional phrasing does not restrict a permission.

## The hadith and the Companions — pages 510 to 515

Ghaylan ibn Salamah had ten wives when he became Muslim. The Prophet, peace be upon him, told him: "Choose four, and separate from the rest." Qays ibn al-Harith had eight, and was told to choose four. Nawfal ibn Mu'awiyah had five, and was told to separate one.

The rightly guided Caliphs: Abu Bakr had more than one wife; at his death one was pregnant, and another, Asma bint Umays, washed his body. Umar had more than one wife at his death. Ali married several after Fatimah.

And there is consensus, ijma', that a man may marry up to four, reported by Sarakhsi, Qurtubi, Ibn Hajar, Ibn Qudamah and others. A few dissenters claimed nine or eighteen, which is rejected.

## The wisdom — pages 515 to 523

The article gives eight reasons.

One, male nature. Ibn al-Qayyim refutes the claim that women's desire exceeds men's.

Two, men's desire for variety. Shah Wali Allah said men's nature inclines to multiple wives, and the Shari'ah limited it to four.

Three, external stimuli. Men face constant arousal outside the home, and one wife may not be enough.

Four, protecting chastity. During the wife's menses, pregnancy, postnatal bleeding or illness, a man may be driven to sin. The West refuses legal polygamy, yet has mistresses and affairs.

Five, men's role of qiwamah, their greater responsibilities. The author adds a footnote: this superiority is in roles, and as human beings men and women have equal rights.

Six, increasing the numbers of the Ummah.

Seven, social necessity. After wars there are many widows and orphans.

Eight, the failure of Western society, with illegitimate children and broken families.

## Why only four? — pages 523 to 524

Before Islam there was no limit. Four allows justice. Ibn al-Qayyim said the limit prevents injustice while still sufficing to protect from zina. And Ashraf Ali Thanawi gave an explanation based on pregnancy: it becomes known after about three months, so with four wives each cycle covers the next, making four a natural limit.

## Why only one husband for a woman? — pages 525 to 527

First, lineage would be confused. There is a story attributed to Abu Hanifah's daughter, who had women each pour milk into one bowl, then asked each to take back her own, which was impossible. The author notes the story itself is doubtful, but the wisdom stands. Second, a woman can carry only one man's child at a time. Third, male jealousy would lead to killing and chaos.

# Section 2: Pakistani society and second marriage — pages 532 to 548

Now the author speaks in his own voice. Second marriage is Sunnah, not fard. Some people are extreme against it, and others promote it as though it were nearly obligatory. It is permitted, and recommended, only when its conditions are met. Otherwise it can bring sin.

## Four conditions — pages 533 to 539

One, sufficient financial means. Each wife has a right to a separate dwelling, as the Prophet gave each wife her own room, and two wives cannot be put in one dwelling without their consent. An average salaried man in Pakistan cannot afford two households. The author gives the example of a worker in a religious movement whose second marriage became a burden on the movement and ruined two homes.

Two, physical ability to fulfil conjugal rights. For example, a sixty year old marrying an eighteen year old will likely fail her rights, though if she freely consents it is not forbidden. Men often seek a second marriage in old age, after the first wife has aged, which often leads to injustice.

Three, it must not be merely for lust. Marriage is a responsibility, "seeking chastity, not lust".

Four, justice between the wives in maintenance, housing, time, nights and gifts. Inner affection is excused. "Whoever has two wives and inclines to one will come on the Day of Resurrection with half his body fallen." The common Pakistani pattern is that the younger second wife is favoured, while the first wife and her children are neglected, even made to do menial work, or divorced. The author tells of a respected religious worker who abandoned his first wife and five or six children for a young second wife. When asked why, he said she was worse than an animal to him. The author replied: "Yet you had five or six children with her!"

## Excuses and wrong analogies — pages 540 to 545

The story of Sawdah, who gave her turn to Aisha, is used to justify discarding older wives. But the author says the report that the Prophet intended to divorce her lacks a sound chain. Sawdah chose to give her day herself.

Some argue that the Prophet married many wives despite poverty, so marriage brings barakah and helps da'wah. The author says his situation was unique, and in Pakistan second marriages usually harm religious work and create family rivalry.

Some point to Arab societies, where polygamy is common. But there, women have independent wealth, large inheritances, their own homes, and separate households. In Pakistan, women depend economically on their husbands, families live together, and inheritance is denied, so a second wife often causes crisis, divorce and destitution. Maulana Abdul Rahman Kilani said that about ninety five percent of men do not need it. Where a man does need it, the permission exists, but Arab practice cannot simply be copied.

The author also condemns a practice among some: keeping four wives and divorcing one simply to marry a new one, staying within the limit. That is lust-driven and blameworthy. Some reportedly cut off one wife without divorcing her in order to marry a fifth, which is even worse. He notes that not all Arabs or Afghans do this.

## Marrying widows — pages 545 to 546

Hindu influence made Muslims look down on marrying widows. The author welcomes the trend of encouraging marriage with the widows of martyrs, and urges that men who want a second marriage should give priority to widows, not virgins, so that widows are not neglected.

## Summary

To sum up Chapter 10: polygamy, up to four wives, is established by the Qur'an, the Sunnah, the practice of the Companions and consensus, and the book defends its wisdom. But the author is firm that a second marriage is Sunnah, not obligatory, and is allowed only with financial means, physical ability, a purpose beyond lust, and real justice between the wives. In Pakistani conditions, he warns, most men do not need it, and it often leads to injustice. And he encourages giving priority to widows. In the next lecture, we come to Islam and birth control.
